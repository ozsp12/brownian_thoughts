For many years, I have heard the expression, “Work in silence.” It is usually delivered with the solemnity of a moral lesson, almost like one of those old German cautionary tales written for children — stories intended to educate through fear and consequence — yet which often resemble, at least in atmosphere, narratives conceived in the horror novels of Stephen King. The phrase carries with it the suggestion that one should protect ideas from the ambitions of others, conceal one’s intentions from the world, and reveal one’s achievements only after success has already been secured and can no longer be threatened.

Yet reality, in my experience, tends to be considerably less theatrical than this romantic image suggests.

More often than not, our ideas are simply not as revolutionary as we imagine them to be during those private moments of intellectual enthusiasm. Alternatively, even when an idea possesses genuine merit, most people lack either the inclination or the capacity to pursue it seriously. Execution is an extraordinarily rare quality. It demands intellectual discipline, persistence over long periods of time, emotional resilience, technical competence, resources, sacrifice, and above all the ability to tolerate uncertainty. Many admire ideas in theory, but very few are willing to endure the long and often tedious process required to transform abstraction into reality.

There is also another possibility, perhaps the most humbling of all: we are not nearly as important as we imagine ourselves to be. Most people are far too occupied with their own concerns, ambitions, insecurities, and routines to dedicate meaningful attention to ours. This is not necessarily depressing. On the contrary, there is something strangely liberating about recognising one’s relative insignificance within the immense scale of society and history.

I frequently observe people sharing fragments of their thoughts, projects, ambitions, and theories across social media platforms. Quite honestly, I regard this as something fundamentally positive, even when those ideas are still immature, incomplete, or imperfectly articulated. Civilisation advances through plurality — through the collision of perspectives, interpretations, disagreements, and experiments. Intellectual stagnation emerges not from the existence of bad ideas, but from the absence of open discourse altogether. Naturally, this plurality must remain bounded by civility, reason, and humanity, otherwise discussion degenerates into noise and hostility rather than genuine intellectual exchange.

Some individuals seek visibility, influence, social prestige, or financial reward through their public contributions. I neither condemn nor romanticise such motivations. Human beings have always desired recognition in one form or another, whether through art, science, politics, literature, or performance. To contribute publicly is also, in some sense, to ask for acknowledgment from the world. I would be dishonest if I claimed never to have considered such things myself.

And yet, what truly attracts me to the act of sharing ideas is something rather different.

There is a peculiar beauty in obscurity. I find comfort in the fact that I occupy no position of great public relevance. I enjoy the sensation of speaking into the void without the burden of performance or expectation. At times, I imagine myself almost like Voyager 1, drifting silently through interstellar darkness while transmitting faint signals into the cosmos, uncertain whether anyone, somewhere, will ever receive them or understand them. There is something deeply poetic about that image to me: communication without certainty, expression without guarantee, thought released into the unknown.

Those who know me personally are well aware that I am not particularly fond of conversation, especially when discussions remain superficial or performative. I have always found casual small talk exhausting. Writing, however, is entirely different. Writing permits precision. It permits structure. It allows thought to mature before being exposed to the world. I enjoy writing essays, reflections, observations, theoretical digressions, and intellectual explorations of almost every kind. Writing feels less like speaking and more like constructing a landscape in which ideas may exist independently of their creator.

Now, with the emergence of artificial intelligence, an entirely new set of creative instruments has become available. For the first time, it is possible for a single individual to produce narrations, visual compositions, dialogues, animations, and entire multimedia projects with tools that would once have required teams of specialists. Regardless of the inevitable fears and exaggerations surrounding artificial intelligence, I cannot deny that I find this technological moment intellectually fascinating.

For this reason, I decided to create a channel that will function, in essence, as a kind of intellectual diary — perhaps somewhere between a podcast, a collection of essays, and a stream of consciousness. It will serve as an archive for a mind that rarely remains still for very long. In most cases, I intend to use AI-generated narration, not merely as a practical convenience, but also because there is something strangely appropriate about employing synthetic voices to articulate reflections on technology, philosophy, science, society, and existence itself.

The name of the channel is “Brownian Thoughts.”

The title is intended as a small conceptual play involving both physics and philosophy. In physics, Brownian motion describes the apparently random movement of particles suspended within a fluid, constantly colliding with invisible molecules in chaotic and unpredictable ways. The metaphor seemed appropriate. Human thought often behaves similarly: restless, erratic, constantly redirected by external stimuli, memories, emotions, ideas, and experiences that collide invisibly within the mind.

Originally, I had considered other names. One possibility was “Beyond the Curtains,” an allusion to Wizard of Oz and the famous revelation of the fragile human figure hidden behind spectacle and illusion. Another possibility was “Behind the Cave,” inspired by the Allegory of the Cave proposed by Plato, perhaps one of the most enduring metaphors ever written about ignorance, perception, and the painful pursuit of truth. Unfortunately — or perhaps inevitably — others had already arrived at those ideas long before I did.

Which, I suppose, is yet another reminder that very few ideas truly belong to us alone.
